\subsection{Digital audio, inputs and outputs of the MDX-Net models (chunks and spectrograms)}%
\label{app:uvr_mdx}

Sounds we hear are continuous waveforms. But in the digital world, audio is \textbf{discretized}. Only a finite number of samples per second of the audio waveform are stored, typically $44100$ samples per second. We note that under certain mathematical conditions, this is sufficient to perfectly reproduce the audio signal (Nyquist-Shannon theorem).

MDX-Net models are fed with \textbf{spectrograms} of audio \textbf{chunks}. A chunk is simply an audio piece, typically a small number of seconds (like $5.9$). Primarily, the audio we hear is in the \textbf{time domain}. The signal is the discretized amplitude of the wave as a function of the time. The \textbf{Fourier transform} allows to compute which \textbf{frequencies} are represented in the signal. We note that the frequency spectrum is also discretized (here, typically we use $2048$ frequency bins, stepped by $10.8$ Hz). Here, spectrograms are a collection of Fourier transforms of audio \textbf{frames}. There are $256$ frames in the chunk, each frame containing $23$ ms of audio. We note that the frames \textbf{overlap}. You can find more information on this topic in the \textbf{my\textunderscore models/src/spectrograms.py} file. In figures \ref{fig:stft_bill} and \ref{fig:stft_grants}, we can see to example spectrograms.
